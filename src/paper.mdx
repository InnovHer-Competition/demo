## Overview

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) affects an estimated 10–13% of women of reproductive age, yet up to 70% of them do not know they have it. Diagnosis is often slow, and the medical history it depends on is intimate: menstrual, sexual, and reproductive. Many patients are reluctant to share that information because of stigma, embarrassment, or fear that their data will be misused.

We present a **privacy-preserving, multi-agent intelligent chatbot** for early PCOS screening and education. Patient screening, workflow automation, and personalised lifestyle recommendations all live in one auditable interface.

## Controllable history-taking agent

We formulate medical history-taking as a **constrained POMDP** with three components: a policy $\pi$ that chooses the next action, an extractor $f$ that turns the conversation into a structured history, and a quality measure $Q$ for that history. Each research question targets one component.

<Cards
  items={[
    {
      icon: "ri:route-line",
      title: "RQ1 – Policy π",
      text: "How should the agent choose its next action?",
      points: ["Cover the PCOS guideline fields", "Follow the communication protocol", "Respect consent and safety"],
    },
    {
      icon: "ri:file-list-3-line",
      title: "RQ2 – Extractor f and quality Q",
      text: "How do we extract and assess a patient history?",
      points: ["Facts, timing and chronology", "Evidence and uncertainty per field", "Coverage and factual accuracy"],
    },
  ]}
/>

The **LLM handles language; state, policy and constraints stay explicit.** A state tracker keeps the known, missing and uncertain fields of the history against a PCOS schema $\mathcal{S}$ and the Calgary–Cambridge consultation steps. The policy picks the next action (ask, clarify, acknowledge, summarize, escalate or stop), and every action is checked against consent, red-flag and escalation rules $\mathcal{C}$ before the LLM phrases it for the patient.

## Why PCOS?

PCOS comes from a disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) axis. Abnormal GnRH secretion raises LH relative to FSH. Together with insulin resistance, this drives excess androgen production in the ovaries, and the resulting hyperandrogenic environment disrupts follicle maturation, often leading to chronic anovulation. Left undiagnosed or poorly managed, PCOS raises long-term risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, endometrial hyperplasia, infertility, and psychological comorbidity.

It is also a **growing public health challenge**:

There is no definitive cure, but **early diagnosis combined with lifestyle changes** (a nutritious diet and regular physical activity) is a low-cost way to limit metabolic complications. Accessible, evidence-based digital tools can raise awareness and shorten the path to diagnosis.

## Design goals

**Facilitate disclosure, not just data collection.** A clinical consultation only works if patients are willing and able to share relevant information. Using a digital agent does not by itself make people disclose more. Trust in the system, transparency about how data is used, and perceived privacy and security are what matter. Our chatbot is designed so that patients can share sensitive information appropriately, while that information stays private, secure, and confidential.

**Explainability as a prerequisite for trust and compliance.** Opaque "black-box" models make clinical adoption harder and raise concerns about bias, fairness, and regulatory compliance. Our system aims for:

<Cards
  columns={3}
  items={[
    { icon: "ri:eye-line", title: "Transparency", text: "Users can see how and why an output was produced, so they can check AI-assisted decisions instead of accepting them blindly." },
    { icon: "ri:history-line", title: "Accountability", text: "Every decision can be traced back to the specific model version that made it." },

These properties support the principles of biomedical ethics (beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice). They also address the transparency and human-oversight requirements of the GDPR, HIPAA, and the EU AI Act, none of which prescribe a specific explainability mechanism.
